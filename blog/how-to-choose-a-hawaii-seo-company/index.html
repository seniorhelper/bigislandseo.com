How to choose a Hawaii SEO company: 12 questions to ask.

The questions that separate a real Hawaii SEO partner from a mainland reseller, with a scorecard to fill in during the call.

The short version

  • Ask who does the work, and make sure they know your island and town, not just "Hawaii."
  • Own everything: domain, website, Google Business Profile, analytics and ad accounts.
  • Walk away from guaranteed rankings. Google says no one can guarantee a #1 spot.
  • Judge reports by calls, forms and bookings, not traffic charts.
  • Use the scorecard below during the sales call.

Choosing a Hawaii SEO company is harder than it should be. The search results for "Hawaii SEO" are full of mainland agencies with a Hawaii page, freelancers with a stock photo of Diamond Head and resellers who pass the work along to someone you will never meet. Some of them are good. Many are not. And from the outside, their sales pitches sound almost identical.

These are the twelve questions we would want a friend or family member to ask before signing with anyone, including us. We have been building and ranking websites since 2012, and almost every painful story we hear from Hawaii owners traces back to one of these questions never being asked.

Why hiring for Hawaii is different

Three things make Hawaii harder than a typical mainland city. First, the state is six separate search markets, and Hawaii Island alone behaves like two. Second, many businesses sell to two audiences, residents and visitors, who search at different times, from different places and with different words. Third, the islands run on their own time zone, their own seasons and their own vocabulary. An agency that has never dealt with these details will still sell you a plan. It just will not be a Hawaii plan.

That is why several of the questions below are specifically about local knowledge. The rest apply anywhere, but they matter more here, because the Hawaii market is small enough that one bad year with the wrong provider can cost a lot of ground.

The 12 questions

Ask them in this order if you can. The first few tell you whether the conversation is worth continuing.

1Who will actually do the work, and where are they?

Many agencies that rank for Hawaii searches resell work to offshore teams or white-label vendors. That is not automatically bad, but you deserve to know who touches your site and your Google Business Profile.

Good answer: A named person or team, how long they have worked on Hawaii accounts, and who you call when something breaks.

Red flag: Vague answers about "our team" or refusal to say where the work is done.

2Do they know the difference between Kona and Hilo?

Hawaii is six markets, and some islands contain two. Kona runs on visitor and resort-corridor demand; Hilo runs on residents. An agency that treats "Hawaii" as one market will build one generic page and wonder why it ranks nowhere.

Good answer: They talk about your island and your town specifically, and ask whether your customers are residents, visitors or both.

Red flag: A plan that could be pasted into any mainland city by changing the name.

3Can they show rankings from where your customers actually are?

Map pack rankings change block by block. A screenshot taken from the agency's own office proves very little about what a customer in Kaneohe or Waimea sees.

Good answer: Location-based ranking reports (often called geo-grids) across your real service area, before and after.

Red flag: Screenshots with no location, or rankings for phrases nobody searches.

4Who owns the website, domain, profile and ad accounts?

This is the question that saves the most heartache. If the agency registered your domain, owns your Google Business Profile or runs ads from its own account, leaving can mean losing assets you paid for.

Good answer: You own everything in your name, with the agency added as a manager or user.

Red flag: "We host it for you" with no clear path to transfer, or an ads account you cannot log into.

5What exactly happens in month one?

"Ongoing optimization" is not a deliverable. A fixed list of work with names and dates is.

Good answer: A written list such as: tracking installed, profile rebuilt, four pages published, twenty citations corrected, with dates.

Red flag: Only activities and adjectives, no outputs.

6How do you report, and what do you count?

Traffic and impressions are easy to inflate. Calls, form requests and bookings are what pay for the retainer.

Good answer: Monthly reports that lead with leads and cost per lead, with rankings as supporting evidence.

Red flag: A 30-page PDF of charts that never mentions a phone call.

7Do they promise a #1 ranking?

Google's own guidance on hiring an SEO says plainly that no one can guarantee a #1 ranking. Algorithms, competitors and search features change constantly.

Good answer: Clear expectations, realistic timelines and a commitment to effort and transparency instead of positions.

Red flag: Guaranteed rankings, guaranteed traffic or "page one in 30 days."

8What is the contract, and what is the exit?

Month-to-month should mean month-to-month. Watch for auto-renewals, setup fees and cancellation fees buried in the terms.

Good answer: Month-to-month with a short notice period and nothing held hostage when you leave.

Red flag: Twelve-month lock-ins for a service that cannot guarantee anything.

9How do they handle AI search?

More customers now ask ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, Perplexity or Google's AI features for recommendations. Being cited there depends on clear, consistent information about your business across the web.

Good answer: A concrete answer about structured data, consistent business details, citable pages and third-party mentions.

Red flag: Either "AI does not matter" or a mysterious AI package with no specifics.

10How do they build links and mentions?

Links and mentions still matter, and the wrong kind can hurt. Paid link schemes and spammy directories can trigger problems that take months to undo.

Good answer: Relevant, real placements: local organizations, suppliers, industry sites and genuinely related Hawaii properties.

Red flag: "We will get you 500 backlinks" or refusal to say where links come from.

11Do they write for Hawaii readers?

Hawaiian place names, local terms and island realities show up in how people search and how AI understands you. Content that spells Kāneʻohe wrong or talks about snow tires tells both people and algorithms something.

Good answer: Writers who know the islands, use correct spelling where it helps and write for how locals and visitors actually speak.

Red flag: Generic content spun for every state.

12What will you tell me if I do not need you?

Good agencies turn work down. If your profile is in great shape and your problem is really your phone answering, a good agency says so.

Good answer: A free audit that stands on its own, even if you never hire them.

Red flag: Every audit concludes you need the biggest package.

Red flags

What Google itself says about hiring an SEO

Google publishes a page for business owners called Do you need an SEO? It is short, practical and worth ten minutes of your time before any sales call. Among other things, it suggests asking providers for examples of past work, how they measure success and how long they have been in business, and it warns that no one can guarantee a top ranking.

That last point is also our own position. We never promise rankings or traffic, because algorithms and markets change overnight. What we do promise is to put your business first, work on it as if it were our own, and bring every tool and skill we have to growing it.

Contracts, ownership and the exit

If you only remember one section, make it this one. Before you sign anything, confirm in writing that:

  • The domain is registered in your name, at a registrar you can log into.
  • You are the owner of your Google Business Profile, with the agency added as a manager.
  • Your Google Analytics property and Google Ads account belong to you, with admin access.
  • Ad spend is billed to your card directly, and the management fee is a separate number.
  • The contract is month-to-month, or has a clear, short exit.
The most common trap: an agency that built your site on its own hosting, in its own account, under a license that ends when you stop paying. Ask what happens to the website the day you leave.

Local knowledge you can test in five minutes

You do not need to be an SEO expert to check whether an agency knows Hawaii. Ask them any of these and listen for specifics:

  • "Should I have one page for the Big Island, or separate pages for Kona and Hilo?"
  • "My customers are mostly visitors. When are they searching for me?"
  • "Should my Google Ads target people in Hawaii, or people interested in Hawaii?"
  • "How should I spell Hawaiian place names on my site?"

Quick answers

How much should a Hawaii SEO company cost?

Most legitimate Hawaii SEO engagements run roughly $1,000 to $5,000 per month depending on the island, competition and scope. Very cheap retainers rarely include enough real work to move rankings in a contested market.

Should I hire a local Hawaii agency or a mainland one?

Location matters less than knowledge. A mainland-based team with real Hawaii experience can outperform a local one without it. Test for island-specific answers rather than an address.

How long before SEO shows results?

Profile and technical fixes can show movement in the first month. Meaningful ranking and lead growth usually takes three to six months and keeps compounding after that.
